Every mineral value chain is a set of relationships. Following them answers the questions that decide whether a project benefits the people closest to it.

## The actors

- **Host communities and Indigenous peoples** live with the land use, water changes, dust, traffic and social change. They may also gain jobs, contracts and services.
- **Workers**, direct employees and contractors, in mines, processing plants, factories, workshops and recycling yards bear occupational risks. Contracted and informal workers often have the least protection.
- **National and local governments** grant rights, set taxes and royalties, regulate the environment and labour, and decide how revenue is used.
- **Companies** explore, mine, process, manufacture and recycle. They range from global firms and state-owned enterprises to small local businesses.
- **Buyers and brands** shape requirements through contracts and standards.
- **Financiers**, such as banks, development finance institutions, investors and insurers, set conditions on money.
- **Civil society, unions, researchers and journalists** document, advocate, represent and hold others to account.

## Five questions to ask of any project

**1. Who decides?** Who granted the licence, and through what process? India's 2023 amendment to its mining law, for example, allows the central government to auction concessions for listed critical minerals, changing which level of government makes that decision. Ask where affected people had a voice, and whether that voice could change the outcome.

**2. Who captures value?** Look at taxes, royalties, profits, wages, local procurement and processing. Indonesia's nickel ore export ban, announced to take effect in January 2020, is a deliberate attempt to keep more processing value in the country. Whether that value reaches workers and nearby communities is a separate question.

**3. Who bears the costs and risks?** These include environmental damage, health and safety hazards, loss of land or livelihoods, and the financial risk if a project fails or prices collapse. Costs that fall on people without a say are the clearest warning sign.

**4. Who pays, through what mechanism, and when?** Public functions such as monitoring, health care, emergency preparedness, closure and rehabilitation need funding. Some come from company obligations, some from public budgets and some from earmarked funds. India's District Mineral Foundations, financed by mining leaseholders, had collected more than ₹1 lakh crore by early 2025. How such funds are spent is itself a governance question.

**5. Do commitments become practice?** Announcements, targets and policies are not outcomes. Follow the chain from commitment to implementation to measured result.

## Individual responsibility and shared coordination

Some responsibilities belong to one actor. An employer must keep its workplace safe. An operator must manage its tailings. Other needs, such as regional water planning, flood warning or skills systems, require coordination among many actors. Coordination never dilutes an individual actor's own obligations. Benefit-sharing schemes never substitute for rights or for remedy when harm occurs.
