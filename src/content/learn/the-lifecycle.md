It is tempting to picture minerals moving in a tidy circle, from mine to product to recycling and back. Reality is a chain with leaks, and every link has its own people and power.

## The stages

**1. Exploration.** Geologists survey, drill and test. Most exploration finds nothing economic. Even so, it already brings outsiders, permits and expectations into a place, often before communities have clear information.

**2. Extraction.** Ore is mined from open pits or underground workings, or brine is pumped from salt flats. Land is cleared, water is used and waste rock and tailings accumulate. Mining also ranges from large industrial sites to artisanal and small-scale mining by individuals and families.

**3. Processing and refining.** Ore is crushed and concentrated, then smelted, leached or chemically refined into metal or chemical compounds. This stage is often energy-intensive and can create air, water and solid-waste hazards. As explainer 2 showed, it is also where supply is most concentrated.

**4. Manufacturing.** Refined materials become components such as cathodes, anodes, magnets and wire, then products such as battery cells, motors, panels and phones.

**5. Use.** Products are used for years or decades. How long they last depends on design, maintenance and how they are used.

**6. Repair and reuse.** Extending a product's life, or giving a battery a second life in less demanding storage, delays the need for new material. Repair and refurbishment are often done by small and informal businesses.

**7. Collection and recycling.** At end of life, products must be collected, dismantled and processed to recover materials. Collection is often the weakest link: what is never collected cannot be recycled.

## Why the loop leaks

Even well-run recycling recovers less than 100% of a material, and some uses disperse materials so thinly that recovery is impractical. Products last a long time, so today's recycling reflects yesterday's smaller sales. Growing demand cannot yet be met from recycled material alone.

Policy is trying to raise recovery. The European Union's Batteries Regulation sets lithium recovery targets of 50% by the end of 2027 and 80% by the end of 2031, and minimum recycled content in new batteries from 2031. India's Battery Waste Management Rules, notified in 2022, introduced extended producer responsibility for collection and recycling.

## Reducing the need for new material

Before recycling, there are earlier levers:

- **Material efficiency:** using less material for the same function.
- **Substitution:** switching to a more abundant material or a different chemistry. Lithium iron phosphate batteries avoid nickel and cobalt, for example.
- **Longer life and repair:** keeping products in use for longer.
- **System choices:** public transport, smaller vehicles or demand reduction change total material needs.

None of these levers is automatic. Each moves work, value and risk to different people. A battery gets a second life, but what happens to the livelihood of the collector who used to recover it?
