"Critical" sounds like a property of the rock. It is not. A mineral is called critical when two things are true at once for a particular economy:

1. **It matters.** It is important for industries, technologies or national goals that the economy cares about.
2. **Its supply is vulnerable.** Supply could be disrupted because production is concentrated in few places, substitutes are scarce, trade could be restricted, or new supply takes many years to develop.

Neither condition is about rarity. Copper is common in the earth's crust compared with many elements, yet it often appears on critical lists because demand is vast and new mines take a long time to open. Rare earth elements are not especially rare geologically either. Their supply risk comes mainly from where and how they are separated and refined.

## Critical for whom, for what, and when?

Because criticality depends on an economy's needs and its supply exposure, lists differ:

- **By country.** India identified 30 critical minerals in its first list, published in 2023. The European Union, the United States and others publish their own lists, built on their own industries and import patterns.
- **By purpose.** A list built for defence differs from one built for clean energy or electronics.
- **Over time.** Lists are revised as technologies, prices and trade change. India's Ministry of Mines said its list would be revisited periodically.

"Critical raw materials" is the European Union's preferred term. The EU also names a narrower group of "strategic" raw materials. Its 2024 law sets benchmarks for how much of its consumption should be mined, processed and recycled within the EU.

## Where vulnerability really sits

For many energy minerals, the tightest point is not the mine but the refinery. Reporting on the International Energy Agency's 2025 outlook says that for copper, lithium, nickel, cobalt, graphite and rare earths, the top three refining countries held about 86% of the market on average in 2024, up from about 82% in 2020.

That concentration is why governments talk about "diversifying" supply. Diversification creates new projects in new places, and that is where this portal's central question begins: when supply moves, who decides, who benefits and who bears the risk?

## What criticality is not

A critical minerals list is a tool of industrial and security policy. It is not a measure of whether a project is just, whether communities consented, or whether more extraction is needed at all. Repair, reuse, efficiency, substitution and recycling can all reduce how much new mining a given level of demand requires. Explainer 3 covers these.
