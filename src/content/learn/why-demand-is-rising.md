Several forces are increasing demand for critical minerals at the same time.

## What is driving demand

**Electricity networks.** Expanding and modernising grids uses large amounts of copper and aluminium for lines, cables and transformers.

**Clean energy technologies.** Solar panels, wind turbines, electric vehicles and battery storage use different minerals in different amounts. Wind turbines and motors may use rare earth magnets. Batteries use lithium plus varying amounts of nickel, cobalt, manganese, graphite and other materials, depending on chemistry. There is no single "clean energy mineral".

**Digital systems.** Data centres, electronics and communications networks add demand for copper and a range of specialty metals.

**Industry and defence.** Many critical minerals have long-standing industrial uses, from steel alloys to catalysts, that continue to grow.

**Supply-security policy.** Governments are building stockpiles, subsidising domestic processing and restricting exports. Recent examples include China's export licensing for certain graphite products from December 2023 and for seven rare earth elements from April 2025, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo's cobalt export measures in 2025. These change where and how fast projects are built.

## Observed trends versus scenarios

It helps to keep two kinds of statement apart.

**Observed trends** describe what has happened, such as production, trade and prices in past years. They can be measured, although data quality varies.

**Scenarios** describe what could happen under stated assumptions about policy, technology, prices and behaviour. The International Energy Agency, for example, publishes several scenarios side by side. A scenario is not a prediction. Its value lies in showing how outcomes change when assumptions change.

When a headline says "demand will grow X-fold by 2040", ask:

- Which scenario does this come from, and what does that scenario assume about climate policy?
- Does it assume today's battery chemistries, or shifts towards chemistries that use different minerals?
- Does it include recycling, efficiency and substitution?
- Is it total demand, or demand from clean energy only?

## Where supply growth is coming from

Recent supply growth has been highly concentrated. The IEA reported that about 90% of recent refined supply growth came from the single largest supplier of each mineral, for example Indonesia for nickel and China for cobalt and graphite. Its 2026 outlook found China and Indonesia together accounted for more than three-quarters of refined supply growth across key energy minerals.

This is why so many governments and companies are seeking new projects elsewhere, including in India and Indonesia's neighbours. Pressure to move quickly is the context in which questions of consent, safety and benefit sharing are decided.
