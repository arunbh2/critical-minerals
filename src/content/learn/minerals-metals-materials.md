Start with something in your pocket. A mobile phone contains dozens of chemical elements. Its wiring and circuit boards use copper. Its battery is usually a lithium-ion cell, and depending on the chemistry it may contain cobalt, nickel, manganese or iron phosphate, with graphite in the negative electrode. Its speaker and vibration motor typically use small magnets made with rare earth elements.

None of these arrive in the phone as rocks. To see how they get there, it helps to separate five words that are often used loosely.

## Five words, five different things

**A mineral** is a naturally occurring solid with a defined chemical make-up and structure, such as chalcopyrite, a copper-iron sulphide. Geologists name minerals; markets usually do not.

**An ore** is rock containing enough of a valuable mineral to be worth mining at current prices and with current technology. Whether rock counts as ore therefore changes with prices, technology and policy. A deposit can become ore, or stop being ore, without anything happening underground.

**A metal** is the element after it has been separated and purified: copper cathode, nickel matte, lithium carbonate or hydroxide. Getting from ore to metal is usually the most energy-intensive and chemically complex part of the journey.

**A material** is what manufacturers buy: an alloy, a battery cathode powder, a magnet, a copper wire. Materials can be made from newly mined metal, recycled metal, or both.

**Raw materials** is the broad policy term, used notably in the European Union, that covers minerals, metals and other non-energy materials that industry depends on.

## Why the distinction matters

The difference between ore and refined metal is enormous. Ore often contains only a small percentage, sometimes a fraction of a percent, of the target metal. The rest is waste rock or tailings that must be stored safely, often for generations. Refining concentrates the metal but uses energy, water and chemicals, and produces its own residues.

So "where minerals come from" has at least two answers: where they are dug up, and where they are processed. These places are often in different countries. The second answer is frequently more concentrated than the first. The next explainer looks at why that matters.

## A cable, a battery, a magnet

Three objects show how varied the picture is:

- **An electricity cable** is mostly copper or aluminium. It is simple, heavy and used in enormous quantities across grids, buildings and vehicles.
- **A battery** is a chemical system. Not all batteries contain nickel or cobalt. Lithium iron phosphate batteries, widely used in electric vehicles and storage, contain neither. Chemistry choices change which minerals are needed, and which communities are affected.
- **A permanent magnet** in a wind turbine generator or electric motor may use neodymium and praseodymium, sometimes with dysprosium or terbium. These belong to the rare earth elements, a family of 17 elements with distinct uses rather than a single mineral.

Keep these three objects in mind. They are the doorways into the rest of the journey.
