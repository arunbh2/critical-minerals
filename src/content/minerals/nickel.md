## What it is

Nickel is a hard, silvery metal that resists corrosion. It is mined from two main ore types: **sulphide** ores, and **laterite** ores formed by tropical weathering, which make up much of Indonesia's resources.

## How it is used

Most nickel goes into stainless steel. A growing share goes into battery cathodes, especially nickel-rich chemistries valued for energy density. Not all batteries need nickel: lithium iron phosphate batteries contain none.

## Lifecycle and geography

Laterite ore can be smelted into nickel pig iron or ferronickel for steel, or processed by high-pressure acid leaching into intermediates suitable for batteries. Indonesia announced a ban on nickel ore exports from January 2020, so that processing would happen in the country.

## Development questions

- **Who captures value?** Processing in-country increases industrial output. How much accrues to Indonesian workers, local enterprises and public revenue, rather than to investors, is a central question.
- **Who bears risk?** Fast-growing industrial parks raise questions about occupational safety, housing for migrant workers, air quality and coastal waters.
- **What powers processing,** with what emissions and local air-quality effects?
- **What happens to residues** from acid leaching, and who decides how they are stored?

## Environment and labour

Workplace safety in processing has drawn scrutiny. On 24 December 2023, a furnace accident at a smelter in the Indonesia Morowali Industrial Park killed around 20 workers according to later reporting. The park operator revised its account of the cause, and labour groups criticised safety arrangements. See the [Indonesia dossier](../../countries/indonesia/).

## Efficiency, reuse, substitution and recycling

Nickel is widely recycled through stainless-steel scrap. Battery recycling can recover nickel at high rates, and EU rules set recovery targets of 90% by the end of 2027 and 95% by the end of 2031. Shifts in battery chemistry can lower nickel demand for batteries, or raise it.
