## What it is

Cobalt is a hard, bluish metal. It is rarely mined on its own and is usually recovered as a by-product of copper mining, as in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, or of nickel processing, including in Indonesia.

## How it is used

Battery cathodes are now the largest use. Cobalt also goes into superalloys for jet engines, cutting tools and catalysts. Battery makers have reduced cobalt content in many cathode designs, and lithium iron phosphate batteries contain none.

## Lifecycle and geography

The Democratic Republic of the Congo produced roughly three-quarters of the world's mined cobalt in 2024. Much refining takes place elsewhere. In 2025 the DRC introduced quotas on cobalt exports, an example of a producer government using its market position.

## Development questions

- **Artisanal and small-scale mining:** Alongside industrial mines, artisanal cobalt mining has been the subject of extensive reporting on child labour and unsafe working conditions. What would it take for these miners to work safely and earn fairly, rather than simply be excluded from supply chains?
- **Due diligence:** Buyers increasingly trace cobalt to source. Does due diligence improve conditions, or does it push risk out of sight?
- **Value:** How much value stays in producing countries when refining happens abroad?

## Environment and labour

The main risks include toxic dust and water contamination near mining and processing sites, collapses and accidents in artisanal pits, and exposure of children and pregnant women to hazardous work.

## Efficiency, reuse, substitution and recycling

Cobalt is valuable and recyclable from batteries and alloys. EU rules set recovery targets of 90% by the end of 2027 and 95% by the end of 2031, and minimum recycled content of 16% in new batteries from 2031. Substituting chemistries reduces demand. Responsible transitions for affected miners should accompany it.
