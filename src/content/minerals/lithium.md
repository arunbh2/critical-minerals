## What it is

Lithium is a soft, light, highly reactive metal. Nearly every rechargeable battery chemistry in common use today relies on it, whichever other metals the battery uses.

## How it is used

Batteries dominate demand, for electric vehicles, grid storage and electronics. Lithium also has long-standing uses in glass, ceramics and greases.

## Technology differences

Battery chemistries differ. Nickel-rich cathodes and lithium iron phosphate cathodes both use lithium, but in different chemical forms and quantities. Sodium-ion batteries, an emerging alternative for some uses, avoid lithium altogether.

## Lifecycle and geography

There are two main routes:

- **Hard rock**, mainly the mineral spodumene, is mined, concentrated and chemically converted, often in another country.
- **Brine** is pumped from beneath salt flats and concentrated, traditionally in evaporation ponds, before being processed into lithium carbonate or hydroxide. Newer direct extraction methods aim to reduce land and water use but are at varying stages of maturity.

Lithium is on India's 2023 critical minerals list, and India has begun auctioning critical mineral blocks.

## Development questions

- **Water in arid basins:** How does brine pumping affect fresh groundwater, wetlands and the people who depend on them? Who monitors this, and is the data public?
- **Consent:** Many lithium-bearing salt flats and hard-rock areas are on lands used by Indigenous peoples and pastoralists. Were they informed and able to influence decisions?
- **Value:** Is lithium exported as concentrate, or refined in the country?

## Environment and labour

The main risks include water and land use, chemical handling in refining, waste disposal, and hazards in hard-rock mining and processing.

## Efficiency, reuse, substitution and recycling

Lithium recovery from used batteries is technically possible and is now required by regulation in some places. The EU sets targets of 50% by the end of 2027 and 80% by the end of 2031. Second-life use of vehicle batteries in stationary storage can delay recycling. Sodium-ion and other chemistries may reduce lithium needs for some applications.
