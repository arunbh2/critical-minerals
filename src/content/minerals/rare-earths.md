## What they are

The rare earth elements are a group of 17: the 15 lanthanides plus scandium and yttrium. They are often grouped as **light** rare earths, such as neodymium and praseodymium, and **heavy** rare earths, such as dysprosium and terbium. Despite the name, several are not geologically rare. They are hard to find in concentrations that are economic to mine, and hard to separate from each other.

## How they are used

Each element has distinct uses. The most consequential for the energy transition are neodymium and praseodymium, sometimes with dysprosium or terbium, in high-strength permanent magnets for electric vehicle motors, wind turbine generators and many electronics. Other rare earths serve as catalysts, polishing compounds and phosphors.

## Lifecycle and geography

Rare earths are mined from several deposit types. They must then be separated from one another through long sequences of chemical processing, which is the hardest and most concentrated stage. In April 2025 China placed seven medium and heavy rare earth elements and related products under export licensing. Rare earth elements are on India's 2023 critical minerals list.

## Development questions

- **Waste:** Some rare earth ores contain naturally radioactive elements such as thorium. Who manages these residues, and for how long?
- **Water and chemicals:** Separation uses large volumes of reagents. What monitoring is public?
- **Siting:** As separation capacity is built elsewhere, who chooses the locations, and with what consent?

## Environment and labour

The main risks include chemical and radioactive residues, water contamination, and worker exposure in mining and separation.

## Efficiency, reuse, substitution and recycling

Motor designs that use fewer or no rare earths exist for some applications, with trade-offs. Recycling magnets from end-of-life products is technically possible but not yet widespread, partly because magnets are hard to extract from products. Design for disassembly would help.
