## Role in value chains

India uses large and growing quantities of critical minerals in electronics, vehicles, renewable energy and grid equipment, and relies heavily on imports for many of them. Government policy now aims to build supply across the lifecycle: exploration and mining at home, mineral assets abroad, processing, manufacturing and recycling.

## Major policy drivers

- **A national list.** India published its first list of 30 critical minerals in June 2023.
- **New auction powers.** A 2023 amendment to the national mining law lets the central government auction concessions for listed critical minerals and creates an exploration licence. Auction revenue goes to state governments. The first tranche of 20 blocks was launched in November 2023.
- **A national mission.** In January 2025 the Cabinet approved the National Critical Mineral Mission: ₹16,300 crore of government outlay, plus ₹18,000 crore expected from public sector undertakings and others, over seven years.
- **Producer responsibility.** The Battery Waste Management Rules of 2022, since amended, require battery producers and importers to finance collection and recycling.

## Affected people and places

Much of India's mineral wealth lies in forested, hilly regions of central and eastern India where Adivasi (Scheduled Tribe) communities live. Constitutional protections for Scheduled Areas, the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act of 1996 and the Forest Rights Act of 2006 give communities specific rights in decisions about land and forests. How these rights apply to new critical-mineral concessions is a key question for each project.

Recycling and repair affect a different group: the many informal workers who collect, dismantle and recover materials from electronic and battery waste, often without protective equipment.

## Decision-making institutions

- **Ministry of Mines:** policy, the critical minerals list and central auctions.
- **State governments:** most concessions, revenue and many regulatory functions.
- **District administrations and District Mineral Foundations:** local spending of mining levies.
- **Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change and pollution control boards:** environmental clearance and waste rules.
- **Gram sabhas (village assemblies) in Scheduled Areas:** consultation and consent functions under specific laws.

## Worker and environmental issues

Key concerns include occupational safety in mining and processing; heat exposure for outdoor and contracted workers; air and water pollution near mines and processing; forest loss; and hazardous conditions in informal recycling. See [decent work and safety](../../issues/decent-work-safety/) and [water, climate and disaster risk](../../issues/water-climate-risk/).

## Funding mechanisms

- **District Mineral Foundations** had collected more than ₹1 lakh crore by early 2025. Analyses reported that roughly half had not been spent. These are public funds with a defined purpose. They are not open grants for external organisations.
- **The National Critical Mineral Mission** is a public programme. No open application route for civil society has been identified.
